Thermal Assessment of Buildings in Extraterrestrial Environments

Developing human habitats on the Moon and Mars: thermal assessment of advanced building envelopes — a hybrid inflatable module shielded by an ISRU sulfur–regolith concrete shell, evaluated under extreme lunar and Martian climates.

Polytechnic of MilanA.Y. 2025/26COMSOL MultiphysicsMATLABISRU · Sulfur concrete
Supervisor: Prof. Ehsan Kamel · Co-supervisors: Prof. Andrea G. Mainini, Prof. Giuliana Iannaccone · Graduation session December 2025

The challenge

Long-duration missions to the Moon and Mars require building envelopes that keep astronauts safe under extreme conditions: huge day–night temperature swings, near-absent convection, intense radiation and poorly-characterised in-situ materials. This thesis asks a focused question — how does a hybrid envelope behave thermally when exposed to these climates?

The studied solution is a dual-layer hybrid: an internal, pressurised inflatable module (a multilayer soft-goods shell derived from NASA TransHab) protected by an external sulfur–regolith concrete shell produced on-site through In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU). Between them, an optional vacuum gap paired with multilayer insulation (MLI) forms the primary thermal barrier.

🌑Part 1 — Environment & materialsContext

Two hostile climates

The envelope must survive two very different environments. The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is no convection and the surface swings between cryogenic nights and scorching days. Mars has a thin CO₂ atmosphere that allows limited convection and milder swings, but adds radiative exchange with a cold sky and dust-storm risk.

The hybrid envelope

The envelope couples a lightweight, foldable interior with a heavy, locally-sourced exterior — lightweight for transport, shielded by in-situ mass. From inside to outside:

Inflatable module
Multilayer soft-goods: inner liner, bladder (gas barrier), Kevlar/Vectran restraint layer, MMOD shield, atomic-oxygen layer
Vacuum gap (10 cm)
Suppresses conduction — heat crosses only by radiation across the gap
Multilayer insulation (MLI)
3–30 metallised sheets; the primary thermal resistance of the whole wall
Sulfur–regolith concrete shell
ISRU-produced, 0.6–2.7 m thick — radiation & micrometeoroid shielding + thermal mass

The inflatable concept draws on NASA's TransHab and BEAM heritage; the concrete is a waterless sulfur binder mixed with lunar/Martian regolith, 3D-printable on site.

🧪Part 2 — Thermal modellingMethodology

Two complementary analyses

Heat transfer through the wall was studied at two levels of fidelity. A steady-state model (solved in MATLAB with non-linear thermal-resistance circuits, using the roots/fsolve functions for the radiative coupling) gives a first quantitative estimate under fixed High and Low thermal-forcing boundary conditions. A dynamic 24-hour model (COMSOL Multiphysics, 1-D transient Fourier equation, 1-hour steps) then captures thermal inertia and time-varying irradiance.

Parametric study — what the configurations are

A configuration is one specific recipe for the envelope: a chosen set of the four parameters that most affect heat transfer — concrete thickness, concrete thermal conductivity, MLI thickness and external concrete emissivity. Rather than testing every possible mix, four combinations (Comb 1–4) were sampled with Latin Hypercube Sampling (a statistical method that spreads samples evenly across the whole parameter space), plus two deliberately extreme recipes that bracket the performance envelope:

💡 The two extremes define best- and worst-case thermal behaviour. The High-Thickness configuration (thickest, least-conductive concrete + thickest MLI + highest emissivity) gives the lowest heat transfer — the best thermal protection. The Low-Thickness configuration (thinnest, most-conductive concrete + thinnest MLI) is the worst case. The transient (dynamic) study focuses on these two to bracket the full range of behaviour, each tested with and without the vacuum gap, for Moon and Mars, under High and Low thermal forcing.

Steady-state results

The headline finding: with the vacuum gap in place, the MLI dominates the wall's thermal resistance — the concrete's own properties become almost negligible for conduction. The vacuum gap + MLI slash the conductive flux to just a few W/m² on both bodies. The charts below put the Moon and Mars side by side for each metric.

📊 Why the curves break and jump. Each profile shows two discontinuities, both physical — not plotting errors. (1) The short gap near the inflatable is the vacuum gap (10 cm): heat crosses it only by radiation, so no temperature is defined inside a medium that isn't there, and the line breaks. (2) The steep jump across the MLI + vacuum band is the thermal decoupling — this assembly insulates so well that almost the entire temperature drop (e.g. ~176 K → 353 K on the lunar exterior at peak) is concentrated here. The shaded bands mark the three regions: Inflatable · MLI + vacuum · Concrete.
💡 Across all cases the heat flux reaching the interior inflatable surface stays at just 3–5 W/m², despite exterior peaks above 1200 W/m² (Moon) and 900 W/m² (Mars). The combined MLI + vacuum-gap assembly provides the decisive thermal decoupling; the concrete shell's thermal mass delays and attenuates the external pulse.

Conclusions

The hybrid inflatable + sulfur–regolith concrete envelope is technically feasible for long-duration lunar and Martian settlements. The vacuum gap + MLI act as the main wall resistance, while the concrete mainly governs how fast heat penetrates — its thermal capacity buffers external swings and shapes HVAC sizing.

A key design insight — valid especially on Mars, where the climate is milder — is that the concrete need not be very thick: 1.5 m (or less) already secures thermal performance, saving in-situ resources, processing energy and construction time. On the Moon, the High-Thickness configuration remains preferred given the extreme irradiance and cryogenic nights, cutting the peak exterior flux from ~1301 to ~778 W/m² (low forcing) and ~1256 to ~624 W/m² (high forcing). The Low-Thickness envelope lets far more heat through.

Limitations & next steps: sparse experimental data on sulfur-concrete conductivity (sulfur-content & temperature dependent), approximated lunar irradiance and Martian convection, a dust-storm scenario, longer-than-24 h stabilisation runs, and a geometrically-resolved habitat rather than a 1-D stratigraphy. The thesis provides a unified steady-state + dynamic framework as a foundation for that future work.
